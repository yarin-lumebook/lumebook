# LumeBook carousel playbook: psychology evidence base

Prepared 6 Oct 2026 for the psychology section of the LumeBook (לומיבוק) Instagram carousel playbook.
Goal of the carousels: move a parent from "I recognise this moment" to "I made a free preview". Constraints: no guilt, fear or false urgency; no therapeutic or developmental promises.

## How this was verified

- Every source below was looked up by DOI or title in OpenAlex, Crossref, PubMed or Europe PMC, and/or opened on the publisher or a university repository page. Authors, year, journal and DOI were confirmed from those records.
- Read-level tag after each source:
  - **[F]** full text or the relevant results section was read.
  - **[A]** abstract read (publisher, PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex or Crossref record).
  - **[M]** metadata confirmed but the abstract was not retrievable (paywall or bot block). Any result quoted for an [M] source is marked **[unverified result]**.
  - **[S]** key result taken from a secondary source (search summary or a citing paper). Treat as lower confidence.
- Evidence tags: **WELL-ESTABLISHED** (large literature, meta-analytic support, survives replication), **PARTIAL** (real but small, moderated, or mostly from one domain or group), **CONTESTED** (meta-analyses or replications disagree, or effect shrinks to near zero after bias correction), **WEAK-POP-PSYCH** (popular in marketing writing, weak or failed in the research).
- "Transfer risk" = how far the evidence is from a parent swiping an Instagram carousel. Low = field data from ads/social media. High = lab memory or choice tasks with students.

## Ten things to take away first

1. The single best-supported engine for LumeBook is **self-reference + psychological ownership**: people remember and value things tied to themselves and things they helped make (self-reference meta d = 0.65; IKEA effect conceptually replicated; "I designed it myself" effect shown across five studies). The free preview is an honest way to use this: the parent co-creates and sees their own child as the hero before paying.
2. **Narrative** is well supported for attitudes and intentions, but effects are modest (r around .17 to .23 in the Braddock & Dillard meta-analysis; transportation correlates .44 with attitudes in van Laer et al.). Build carousels as a short, believable family story, not a list of claims.
3. **Peak-end** has strong meta-analytic support (r = .58) and direct ad evidence (Baumgartner et al. 1997): put the emotional high near the end and make the last slide warm and clear.
4. **Curiosity gaps** drive clicks, but the learning and lasting-engagement benefits show up when the gap is actually resolved; clickbait-style gaps may lower perceived credibility, and in large field data industry headline advice does not reliably predict which way an effect goes. Ask the parent's real question on slide 1 and answer it inside the carousel.
5. The popular **Zeigarnik effect** (better memory for unfinished things) failed in a 2025 meta-analysis (recall ratio 0.99). The related **Ovsiankina effect** (people tend to resume interrupted tasks, about 67% of the time) holds up. Use "pick up where you left off" for a saved preview, not cliffhanger tricks.
6. **Loss framing, scarcity, nudges and choice-overload rules** are weaker than marketing lore says: gain vs loss framing differences are tiny (r = .03 / -.04), nudge effects shrink to near zero after publication-bias correction, and the average choice-overload effect is near zero. LumeBook gives up little by banning false urgency.
7. **Faces** raise likes and comments on Instagram (observational: +38% likes, +32% comments), but a 2021 JMR study found product-in-hand images without faces drove more purchase intent and click-through than selfies. Use faces for engagement slides and a "book in a parent's hands" shot for the CTA slide.
8. **Personalised children's books**: a small literature (mostly one UK research group plus a 2025 Swiss experiment) shows more engagement, and in some studies better word learning or story comprehension. One careful study found no difference. Nothing tests effects on coping with transitions. Say "children love seeing themselves in the story", never "proven to help your child cope".
9. **Social Stories (Carol Gray)** evidence is almost all autism research, mostly single-case designs, with "low to questionable" overall effects in an early meta-analysis and a null primary outcome in a 2025 UK RCT. Evidence for typically developing children is thin. Do not use the term or borrow its claims.
10. **Bibliotherapy** evidence for anxiety comes from structured parent-led CBT programmes, not picture books. Shared reading and parent-child talk about feelings is the honest ground: books are a good context for conversations about thoughts and feelings, and guided parents talk more about them. Say "a gentle way to start the conversation", not "reduces anxiety".

# Part 1. Principles

Format for each: definition, tag, domain and transfer risk, effect size, carousel use, where it becomes manipulative, sources.

## 1. Curiosity gap (information-gap theory)

- **Definition:** Curiosity is a felt deprivation caused by noticing a specific gap between what you know and what you want to know; people act to close it.
- **Tag:** Theory: WELL-ESTABLISHED. Use as a headline/hook tactic: PARTIAL, with documented trust costs when it turns into clickbait.
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Mixed. Theory and mechanism from lab trivia tasks (high transfer risk). Headline studies use real platform click data (low transfer risk for slide 1), but outcome is clicks, not trust or purchase.
- **Effect size:** No pooled effect size for curiosity headlines. Field: headline-language effects in Upworthy A/B tests are real, but prior research and industry advice do not reliably predict their direction (Banerjee & Urminsky 2025). WeChat data: curiosity cues predict clicks, but more clicks correlated slightly negatively with likes-per-click (Qiu & Golman 2024).
- **Carousel use:** Slide 1 names a real, specific parent question ("How do you tell a 3-year-old a baby is coming?"). Slides 2 to 6 actually answer it. Menon & Soman (2002) found curiosity ads beat detailed-information ads for interest and learning when they gave a hint and an assured resolution.
- **Manipulative when:** the payoff is withheld or never delivered (e.g., "comment BOOK to get the answer"), the hook overpromises ("the one trick that ends tantrums"), or the gap is created by anxiety ("the mistake you are making with your child"). Clickbait framing may lower perceived credibility (Molyneux & Coddington 2020), although one experiment found no trust effect (Munger et al. 2020).

**Sources**
- Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. *Psychological Bulletin, 116*(1), 75-98. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75. Theoretical review. Key result: curiosity as cognitively induced deprivation from a perceived information gap; intense, transient, tends to disappoint when satisfied. [A]
- Kang, M. J., Hsu, M., Krajbich, I. M., Loewenstein, G., McClure, S. M., Wang, J. T., & Camerer, C. F. (2009). The wick in the candle of learning. *Psychological Science, 20*(8), 963-973. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x. fMRI + behavioural studies with trivia questions. Higher curiosity: more caudate activity, more willingness to pay tokens/time for answers, better recall of surprising answers 1 to 2 weeks later. [A]
- Menon, S., & Soman, D. (2002). Managing the power of curiosity for effective web advertising strategies. *Journal of Advertising, 31*(3), 1-14. https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2002.10673672. Simulated internet ad experiment with clickstream. Curiosity strategy increased interest and learning vs detailed product info; recommends a hint plus assurance that the answer will be given. [A]
- Banerjee, A., & Urminsky, O. (2025). The language that drives engagement: A systematic large-scale analysis of headline experiments. *Marketing Science, 44*(3), 566-592. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2021.0018. Thousands of Upworthy A/B tests. Textual cues matter, but prior research and industry advice "does not always" predict the direction. [A]. Reported specific finding that question headlines reduce click-through [S, not confirmed in primary text].
- Qiu, J., & Golman, R. (2024). Curiosity in news consumption. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 38*(2). https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.4195. 100,000+ WeChat articles, NLP measures. Salient questions, importance and surprise predict clicks; small negative correlation between clicks and likes-to-clicks ratio, i.e., curiosity drives short-term consumption but not necessarily lasting engagement. [A]
- Scott, K. (2021). You won't believe what's in this paper! Clickbait, relevance and the curiosity gap. *Journal of Pragmatics, 175*, 53-66. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2020.12.023. Corpus + pragmatic analysis. Clickbait overuses definite references and superlatives that open "conceptual files" with no content. [A]
- Blom, J. N., & Hansen, K. R. (2015). Click bait: Forward-reference as lure in online news headlines. *Journal of Pragmatics, 76*, 87-100. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2014.11.010. Headline corpus analysis of forward-referencing ("This is why..."). [M] [unverified result]
- Molyneux, L., & Coddington, M. (2020). Aggregation, clickbait and their effect on perceptions of journalistic credibility and quality. *Journalism Practice, 14*(4), 429-446. https://doi.org/10.1080/17512786.2019.1628658. Two online experiments. Clickbait headlines may lower perceived credibility and quality. [A]
- Munger, K., Luca, M., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J. (2020). The (null) effects of clickbait headlines on polarization, trust, and learning. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 84*(1), 49-73. https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfaa008. Two experiments (Facebook-recruited + MTurk). Reading clickbait did not change trust in media, retention or polarisation. Counterpoint: trust damage is not guaranteed in a single exposure. [A]

## 2. Zeigarnik effect and Ovsiankina effect

- **Definition:** Zeigarnik: unfinished tasks are remembered better than finished ones. Ovsiankina: people tend to spontaneously resume interrupted tasks.
- **Tag:** Zeigarnik (memory): WEAK-POP-PSYCH / CONTESTED. Ovsiankina (resumption): PARTIAL (consistent but mostly old lab studies).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Lab task-interruption studies, mostly 1920s to 1980s. High transfer risk; no ad or social media evidence found.
- **Effect size:** Ghibellini & Meier (2025): ratio of recalled interrupted to completed tasks = 0.99 (37 publications, excluding Zeigarnik's own data), i.e., no memory advantage; a small d_z = 0.15 only in the 8 publications where d was computable. Resumption rate about 67% (66.79% excluding Ovsiankina's original; 20 publications), above a 50% baseline.
- **Carousel use:** Do not rely on "open loops make people remember". The defensible use is Ovsiankina-style: if a parent starts a preview, make it easy to resume ("your preview is saved, continue when you're ready"). In carousels, a visible "1/7" progress cue is fine for orientation.
- **Manipulative when:** artificial cliffhangers that force a follow or DM to "finish", fake progress bars, or "you left your child's book unfinished" nag messages that lean on parental guilt.

**Sources**
- Ghibellini, R., & Meier, B. (2025). Interruption, recall and resumption: A meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 12*, Article 962. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w. Meta-analysis of 1,349 screened publications. No memory advantage for unfinished tasks; general tendency to resume them; Zeigarnik effect may depend on experimenter authority, task involvement and individual differences. [F, results section]

## 3. Processing fluency

- **Definition:** The felt ease of processing information (clear type, simple words, familiarity) is used as a cue: fluent things feel truer, more likeable and easier to do.
- **Tag:** Fluency effects on judgments: WELL-ESTABLISHED. "Disfluency makes people think harder / learn better": CONTESTED (largely failed).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Mostly lab (students, trivia, fonts). Medium transfer risk; the mechanism plausibly applies to reading slides.
- **Effect size:** Song & Schwarz (2008) Study 1: same exercise instructions in a hard font were estimated to take 15.1 vs 8.23 minutes (d = 0.9) and willingness to do it dropped (d = 0.95), but with only 20, 27 and 19 participants across three studies. No large direct replication was found in this search. Disfluency: pooling the famous font study with 16 replications found no effect on problem solving (Meyer et al. 2015). Truth effect (repetition makes statements feel truer) is meta-analytically supported across 51 studies (Dechêne et al. 2010).
- **Carousel use:** Large readable Hebrew type, high contrast, one idea per slide, plain words. Make the action look as easy as it is: "photo, name, age, free preview" as three simple visual steps.
- **Manipulative when:** fluency or repetition is used to make unsupported claims feel true (the truth effect works on false statements too), e.g., repeating "proven to ease the transition" across posts.

**Sources**
- Alter, A. L., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2009). Uniting the tribes of fluency to form a metacognitive nation. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 13*(3), 219-235. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309341564. Review. Fluency from many sources (perceptual, conceptual, linguistic) produces similar judgment effects, e.g., higher judged truth. [A]
- Song, H., & Schwarz, N. (2008). If it's hard to read, it's hard to do: Processing fluency affects effort prediction and motivation. *Psychological Science, 19*(10), 986-988. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02189.x. Three small experiments (n = 20, 27, 19). Hard-to-read instructions led to higher effort estimates and lower willingness. [F]
- Meyer, A., Frederick, S., Burnham, T. C., Guevara Pinto, J. D., Boyer, T. W., Ball, L. J., Pennycook, G., Ackerman, R., et al. (2015). Disfluent fonts don't help people solve math problems. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 144*(2), e16-e30. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000049. Original + 16 replications pooled. No disfluency benefit under any condition. [A]
- Xie, H., Zhou, Z., & Liu, Q. (2018). Null effects of perceptual disfluency on learning outcomes in a text-based educational context: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 30*(3), 745-771. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x. [M] Title states null effects.
- Weißgerber, S. C., Brunmair, M., & Rummer, R. (2021). Null and void? Errors in meta-analysis on perceptual disfluency and recommendations to improve meta-analytical reproducibility. *Educational Psychology Review, 33*(3), 1221-1247. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1. Re-check of Xie et al.: transfer null result "rather robust", recall and moderator results doubtful. [A]
- Dechêne, A., Stahl, C., Hansen, J., & Wänke, M. (2010). The truth about the truth: A meta-analytic review of the truth effect. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 14*(2), 238-257. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309352251. 51 studies. Repetition increases judged truth; discussed as a fluency effect. [A]

## 4. Specificity / concreteness

- **Definition:** Concrete language (tangible, specific, easy to picture) is processed more easily and signals that the speaker understands the listener's situation.
- **Tag:** PARTIAL (strong multi-method paper, but one main marketing paper and a customer-service setting).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Field text analysis of 1,000+ real customer-employee interactions plus experiments. Medium transfer risk (service conversations, not social posts).
- **Effect size:** Not summarised as one number in the abstract; concreteness raised satisfaction, purchase intent and actual purchases.
- **Carousel use:** "Noa, 3, is giving her pacifier to the pacifier fairy on Friday" beats "help your child through big changes". Show concrete pages of the book, the actual sentence a parent might read, the bedtime setting.
- **Manipulative when:** concreteness is fabricated (invented customer names, invented numbers, false precision like "87% of parents saw results").

**Sources**
- Packard, G., & Berger, J. (2021). How concrete language shapes customer satisfaction. *Journal of Consumer Research, 47*(5), 787-806. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaa038. Five studies incl. text analysis of 1,000+ real interactions in two field settings. Concrete language increased satisfaction, willingness to purchase and purchases; mediated by "the employee is listening". [A]

## 5. Social proof

- **Definition:** People use what similar others do or say as evidence of what is good or normal.
- **Tag:** General effect of reviews/eWOM on sales: WELL-ESTABLISHED. Specific "descriptive norm message" wording effects (hotel towels): PARTIAL (one failed replication, one supportive, a debated synthesis).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** eWOM meta-analyses use real sales data (low transfer risk). Towel studies are field experiments but a different behaviour.
- **Effect size:** eWOM-sales correlation .091 across 1,532 effect sizes (Babić Rosario et al.); volume elasticity .236, valence elasticity .417 (You et al.). On social media platforms eWOM works better when receivers can judge their similarity to the sender (Babić Rosario et al.).
- **Carousel use:** Real parent UGC and quotes from people like the viewer (Israeli parents of 2 to 6 year olds), real counts only, the parent's own words about a specific moment. Similar-other proof beats big generic numbers.
- **Manipulative when:** fake or incentivised-but-undisclosed reviews, inflated counts, or norms used to shame ("most caring parents already...").

**Sources**
- Goldstein, N. J., Cialdini, R. B., & Griskevicius, V. (2008). A room with a viewpoint: Using social norms to motivate environmental conservation in hotels. *Journal of Consumer Research, 35*(3), 472-482. https://doi.org/10.1086/586910. Two hotel field experiments. Descriptive-norm signs beat the standard environmental sign; "guests in this room" norm strongest. [A]
- Bohner, G., & Schlüter, L. E. (2014). A room with a viewpoint revisited: Descriptive norms and hotel guests' towel reuse behavior. *PLoS ONE, 9*(8), e104086. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0104086. Replication in two German hotels (N = 724; N = 204). Any message beat no message, but norm messages were not better than the standard message; proximity effects inconsistent. [A]
- Reese, G., Loew, K., & Steffgen, G. (2014). A towel less: Social norms enhance pro-environmental behavior in hotels. *Journal of Social Psychology, 154*(2), 97-100. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224545.2013.855623. Two hotels. Provincial-norm messages reduced towel use vs standard message (supportive replication). [A]
- Scheibehenne, B., Jamil, T., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2016). Bayesian evidence synthesis can reconcile seemingly inconsistent results: The case of hotel towel reuse. *Psychological Science, 27*(7), 1043-1046. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616644081. Pooled 7 studies; reported support for descriptive-norm effect [S]. Critiqued by Carlsson, Schimmack, Williams & Bürkner (2017), *Psychological Science, 28*(11), 1694-1697, https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616684682, arguing pooled-data Bayes factors are no substitute for meta-analysis; authors replied with a model-averaging analysis (https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617724426). [M for all three; reply content not read]
- Babić Rosario, A., Sotgiu, F., De Valck, K., & Bijmolt, T. H. A. (2016). The effect of electronic word of mouth on sales: A meta-analytic review of platform, product, and metric factors. *Journal of Marketing Research, 53*(3), 297-318. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.14.0380. 1,532 effect sizes, 96 studies. Mean r = .091; volume matters more than valence; homophily cues matter on social media. [A]
- You, Y., Vadakkepatt, G. G., & Joshi, A. M. (2015). A meta-analysis of electronic word-of-mouth elasticity. *Journal of Marketing, 79*(2), 19-39. https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.14.0169. 51 studies. Volume elasticity .236, valence .417; stronger for privately consumed, low-trialability products. [A]
- Floyd, K., Freling, R., Alhoqail, S., Cho, H. Y., & Freling, T. (2014). How online product reviews affect retail sales: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Retailing, 90*(2), 217-232. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2014.04.004. 26 studies, 443 sales elasticities. Abstract gives no pooled numbers. [A]
- Cialdini, R. B., & Goldstein, N. J. (2004). Social influence: Compliance and conformity. *Annual Review of Psychology, 55*, 591-621. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.142015. Review of social proof, commitment, authority. [M] Cialdini's *Influence* is a trade book, not peer-reviewed.

## 6. Authority / source credibility

- **Definition:** Messages from sources seen as expert and trustworthy persuade more, on average.
- **Tag:** WELL-ESTABLISHED main effect, heavily moderated.
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Decades of lab persuasion studies + marketing meta-analyses incl. eWOM. Low to medium transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** Ismagilova et al. (2020, 20 papers): expertise, trustworthiness and homophily all significantly predict perceived usefulness, credibility and purchase intention in eWOM. Pornpitakpan (2004): high credibility usually wins, but can be a liability under some conditions. Wilson & Sherrell (1993) numbers not retrieved.
- **Carousel use:** Named, real collaborators (e.g., a kindergarten teacher or child psychologist) speaking within their competence: "how I explain a new sibling to 3-year-olds", not "endorses LumeBook as therapy". Disclose paid partnerships. Parent peers are a form of credibility too (homophily).
- **Manipulative when:** implied or fabricated endorsements, anonymous "experts recommend", white-coat imagery, or using a professional to imply clinical or developmental benefits the evidence does not support.

**Sources**
- Pornpitakpan, C. (2004). The persuasiveness of source credibility: A critical review of five decades' evidence. *Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 34*(2), 243-281. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2004.tb02547.x. Review. High-credibility sources generally more persuasive; interactions with message, receiver and channel variables. [A]
- Wilson, E. J., & Sherrell, D. L. (1993). Source effects in communication and persuasion research: A meta-analysis of effect size. *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 21*(2), 101-112. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02894421. [M] [unverified result]
- Ismagilova, E., Slade, E., Rana, N. P., & Dwivedi, Y. K. (2020). The effect of characteristics of source credibility on consumer behaviour: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 53*, 101736. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2019.01.005. 20 papers on eWOM. Expertise, trustworthiness, homophily all matter. [A]
- Amos, C., Holmes, G., & Strutton, D. (2008). Exploring the relationship between celebrity endorser effects and advertising effectiveness: A quantitative synthesis of effect size. *International Journal of Advertising, 27*(2), 209-234. https://doi.org/10.1080/02650487.2008.11073052. Negative celebrity information very damaging; trustworthiness, expertise, attractiveness most influential. [A]
- Biswas, D., Biswas, A., & Das, N. (2006). The differential effects of celebrity and expert endorsements on consumer risk perceptions. *Journal of Advertising, 35*(2), 17-31. https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2006.10639231. Three studies. Experts reduce perceived risk more than celebrities for high-tech products. [A]

## 7. Loss aversion and gain vs loss framing

- **Definition:** Losses are weighted more than equal gains. In messaging, "what you lose if you don't" vs "what you gain if you do".
- **Tag:** Loss aversion in risky choice: WELL-ESTABLISHED on average but CONTESTED as a general law. Loss-framed messages as a persuasion lever: WEAK (differences near zero).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Economic lab gambles (high transfer risk); framing meta-analyses use health messages (medium).
- **Effect size:** Mean loss-aversion coefficient 1.955 (95% interval 1.82 to 2.10) across 607 estimates (Brown et al. 2024). Framing: gain framing slightly better for prevention, r = .03 (93 studies); loss framing slightly better for detection, r = -.04 (53 studies); no difference for sun protection (33 studies).
- **Carousel use:** Lead with gains that are true ("a calm, playful way to talk about the new baby"). No need for loss framing; the evidence says it buys almost nothing.
- **Manipulative when:** "don't let your child fall behind", "every day you wait...", "last chance", countdown timers that reset. These also collide with the brand's no-fear rule.

**Sources**
- Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). Prospect theory: An analysis of decision under risk. *Econometrica, 47*(2), 263-291. https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185. Value function steeper for losses. [A]
- Gal, D., & Rucker, D. D. (2018). The loss of loss aversion: Will it loom larger than its gain? *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 28*(3), 497-516. https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1047. Critical review: evidence does not support losses being generally more impactful than gains. [A]
- Mrkva, K., Johnson, E. J., Gächter, S., & Herrmann, A. (2020). Moderating loss aversion: Loss aversion has moderators, but reports of its death are greatly exaggerated. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 30*(3), 407-428. https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1156. Five samples, N = 17,720. Loss aversion present at all knowledge levels; lower with domain knowledge, higher with age. [A]
- Brown, A. L., Imai, T., Vieider, F. M., & Camerer, C. F. (2024). Meta-analysis of empirical estimates of loss aversion. *Journal of Economic Literature, 62*(2), 485-516. https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20221698. 607 estimates, 150 articles. Mean λ = 1.955. [A]
- O'Keefe, D. J., & Jensen, J. D. (2007). The relative persuasiveness of gain-framed and loss-framed messages for encouraging disease prevention behaviors: A meta-analytic review. *Journal of Health Communication, 12*(7), 623-644. https://doi.org/10.1080/10810730701615198. 93 studies, N = 21,656. Gain advantage r = .03, driven by dental hygiene. [A]
- O'Keefe, D. J., & Jensen, J. D. (2009). The relative persuasiveness of gain-framed and loss-framed messages for encouraging disease detection behaviors: A meta-analytic review. *Journal of Communication, 59*(2), 296-316. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2009.01417.x. 53 studies, N = 9,145. Loss advantage r = -.04, only for breast cancer detection. [A]
- O'Keefe, D. J., & Wu, D. (2012). Gain-framed messages do not motivate sun protection. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 9*(6), 2121-2133. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph9062121. k = 33, N = 4,168. No difference. [A]

## 8. Commitment and consistency (foot-in-the-door)

- **Definition:** After agreeing to a small request, people are somewhat more likely to agree to a larger related one.
- **Tag:** PARTIAL (replicable but weak and inconsistent).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Field and lab compliance requests (door-to-door, phone). Medium to high transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** r = .17 for FITD (Dillard, Hunter & Burgoon 1984); "replicable, weak, not nearly as robust as assumed", nearly half of studies null or reversed (Beaman et al. 1983). Works mainly for prosocial requests; an incentive for the first step undermines it.
- **Carousel use:** A ladder of genuinely useful micro-steps: save the carousel, tap the milestone, upload a photo, see the free preview. Each step must deliver value on its own, so the ladder works even if consistency pressure does nothing.
- **Manipulative when:** sunk-cost traps ("you've already started your child's book, don't give up now"), demanding lots of data before showing the preview, then pressuring at the paywall.

**Sources**
- Freedman, J. L., & Fraser, S. C. (1966). Compliance without pressure: The foot-in-the-door technique. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 4*(2), 195-202. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0023552. Two field experiments. Small first request raised compliance with large second request. [A]
- Beaman, A. L., Cole, C. M., Preston, M., Klentz, B., & Steblay, N. M. (1983). Fifteen years of foot-in-the-door research: A meta-analysis. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 9*(2), 181-196. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167283092002. 120 experimental groups. Weak; nearly half of studies null or wrong direction. [A]
- Dillard, J. P., Hunter, J. E., & Burgoon, M. (1984). Sequential-request persuasive strategies: Meta-analysis of foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face. *Human Communication Research, 10*(4), 461-488. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.1984.tb00028.x. FITD r = .17, DITF r = .15; both need prosocial topics. [A]
- Burger, J. M. (1999). The foot-in-the-door compliance procedure: A multiple-process analysis and review. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 3*(4), 303-325. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0304_2. Several processes (self-perception, consistency, reactance) explain successes and failures. [A]
- No recent large-scale replication was found within the search budget.

## 9. Self-reference effect

- **Definition:** Information related to the self is processed more deeply and remembered better; in ads, prompting people to relate a message to themselves increases elaboration.
- **Tag:** Memory effect: WELL-ESTABLISHED. Persuasion effect: PARTIAL (depends on argument strength). Personalisation in marketing: PARTIAL (positive but small on average).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Memory: lab, mostly undergraduates (82% of studies), but also shown in 4 to 6 year olds. Persuasion: ad-copy experiments. Name personalisation: large field experiments (low transfer risk).
- **Effect size:** Self vs semantic encoding d = 0.65 (60 studies, Symons & Johnson, as reported by Bentley et al. 2017). Adding the recipient's name to an email subject line: opens 9.05% to 10.80% (+20%), leads +31%, unsubscribes -17% (Sahni et al. 2018). Tailored print health messages: r = .074 (57 studies, Noar et al.).
- **Carousel use:** This is the product. Second-person copy ("your child's name on the cover"), show a preview with a child's name and look-alike illustration (with consent), "imagine reading this with [name] tonight". Children themselves also show self-reference advantages, which supports why kids enjoy being the hero (Cunningham et al. 2014).
- **Manipulative when:** it implies surveillance ("we know your child is struggling"), uses real children's images without consent, or turns self-relevance into pressure ("your child deserves better than a generic book").

**Sources**
- Symons, C. S., & Johnson, B. T. (1997). The self-reference effect in memory: A meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin, 121*(3), 371-394. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371. 129 studies. Self-referent encoding beats semantic and other-referent encoding; smaller vs other-reference. [A]; d = 0.65 vs semantic [S: from Bentley et al. 2017].
- Bentley, S. V., Greenaway, K. H., & Haslam, S. A. (2017). An online paradigm for exploring the self-reference effect. *PLoS ONE, 12*(5), e0176611. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0176611. Four online (MTurk) studies replicating the effect: d = 0.69 (0.49 conservative). [F, relevant sections]
- Cunningham, S. J., Brebner, J. L., Quinn, F., & Turk, D. J. (2014). The self-reference effect on memory in early childhood. *Child Development, 85*(2), 808-823. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12144. Three studies, 4 to 6 year olds (N = 53, 55, 56). Objects paired with the child's own image were remembered better. [A]
- Burnkrant, R. E., & Unnava, H. R. (1995). Effects of self-referencing on persuasion. *Journal of Consumer Research, 22*(1), 17-26. https://doi.org/10.1086/209432. Two ad-copy experiments. - Sahni, N. S., Wheeler, S. C., & Chintagunta, P. (2018). ## 10. Narrative transportation

- **Definition:** Being absorbed into a story ("transported") reduces counter-arguing and shifts beliefs and attitudes toward the story.
- **Tag:** WELL-ESTABLISHED, effects modest.
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Mixed lab and marketing studies; van Laer's meta is consumer-focused. Low to medium transfer risk. Note: van Laer's numbers are correlations between measured transportation and outcomes, not causal effects of "using a story".
- **Effect size:** Exposure to narrative vs non-narrative: beliefs r = .17, attitudes r = .19, intentions r = .17, behaviours r = .23 (Braddock & Dillard 2016). Transportation correlates with attitudes ρ = .44, intentions ρ = .31, affective responses ρ = .57, critical thoughts ρ = -.20; antecedents: identifiable characters ρ = .20, imaginable plot ρ = .29, verisimilitude ρ = .27 (van Laer et al. 2014). Health narratives overall r = .063; print narratives not significant (Shen et al. 2015).
- **Carousel use:** Build the carousel as a believable micro-story: a named child, a real moment (the night before kindergarten), what the parent tried, how the book opened a conversation, the child's reaction. Identifiable character + imaginable plot + lifelikeness are the measured levers.
- **Manipulative when:** "true stories" that are invented, or using the lowered critical thinking of a good story to slip in claims ("after one reading, the fear was gone").

**Sources**
- Green, M. C., & Brock, T. C. (2000). Numbers above. [F, accepted-manuscript version from City, University of London repository; published numbers may differ slightly]

## 11. Mere exposure

- **Definition:** Repeated exposure to a stimulus tends to increase liking, up to a point.
- **Tag:** WELL-ESTABLISHED, with an inverted-U (too much repetition reduces liking).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Lab (visual stimuli) plus ad-repetition experiments. Medium transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** Inverted-U across 268 curve estimates (Montoya et al. 2017). In ad experiments, attitude peaks at about 10 exposures; recall rises linearly to at least 8 (Schmidt & Eisend 2015). Bornstein's 1989 pooled effect not retrieved [unverified].
- **Carousel use:** A recognisable series format (same cover template, colours, character style, "Milestone Monday") so the brand becomes familiar. Vary content inside a consistent frame to avoid wear-out.
- **Manipulative when:** aggressive retargeting of parents who viewed a preview containing their child's photo; repetition of unverified claims (overlaps with the truth effect).

## 12. Cognitive load and multimedia principles

- **Definition:** Working memory is limited; design that removes unnecessary processing (clutter), breaks content into parts (segmenting) and highlights what matters (signalling) helps people understand.
- **Tag:** WELL-ESTABLISHED for instructional material; PARTIAL as an ad-performance lever (not tested on carousels).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Learning experiments (students learning explanations). Medium to high transfer risk, but a carousel is literally a segmented, self-paced explanation.
- **Effect size:** Meta-analyses exist for segmenting (Rey et al. 2019), signalling (Richter et al. 2016; Alpizar et al. 2020: 44 effect sizes, 29 studies, 2,726 participants, "small to large") and seductive details (Rey 2012). Exact pooled numbers could not be opened [unverified]. Signalling works best for learners with low prior knowledge [S: Semantic Scholar summary of Richter et al.]. Critique: cognitive load is hard to measure and the theory risks being unfalsifiable (de Jong 2010).
- **Carousel use:** One idea per slide (segmenting), bold the one key phrase (signalling), cut decorative elements that do not carry meaning (coherence), put text near the image it refers to (contiguity). Self-paced swiping fits the segmenting principle.
- **Manipulative when:** the same principles are reversed to hide information: price, the data-use note about the child's photo, or subscription terms buried in clutter or in a slide nobody reaches.

## 13. Peak-end rule

- **Definition:** People judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and its end, with little weight on duration.
- **Tag:** WELL-ESTABLISHED.
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Mostly experienced episodes (pain, events); one ad study shows the same pattern for moment-to-moment ad emotions. Medium transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** Peak-end correlation with retrospective evaluations r = .581 across 174 effect sizes; duration had minimal impact (Alaybek et al. 2022). Ads: overall ad judgments dominated by the emotional peak and the final moment, plus whether feelings improve over time (Baumgartner et al. 1997).
- **Carousel use:** Build toward an emotional high near the end (the child's face seeing themselves as the hero, a short parent quote), then a calm, clear last slide: what the parent gets, that the preview is free, one CTA. Rising arc beats a front-loaded one.
- **Manipulative when:** a feel-good ending is used to cover a bad middle (hidden cost, unclear photo use) or the "peak" is an engineered emotional low (a crying child) to make relief feel bigger.

**Sources**
- Kahneman, D., Fredrickson, B. L., Schreiber, C. A., & Redelmeier, D. A. (1993). When more pain is preferred to less: Adding a better end. *Psychological Science, 4*(6), 401-405. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x. Cold-pressor experiment; majority chose to repeat the longer trial with a less painful end. [A]
- Alaybek, B., Dalal, R. S., Fyffe, S., Aitken, J. A., Zhou, Y., Qu, X., Roman, A., & Baines, J. I. (2022). All's well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 170*, 104149. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149. 174 effect sizes; r = .581; peak and end comparable to the average, outweigh trend and variability; duration minimal. [A, via EconPapers]
- Baumgartner, H., Sujan, M., & Padgett, D. (1997). Patterns of affective reactions to advertisements: The integration of moment-to-moment responses into overall judgments. *Journal of Marketing Research, 34*(2), 219-232. https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379703400203. Three studies with ads; peak + end + improving trend dominate overall ad judgments; duration weak. [A]

## 14. Choice architecture, nudges and choice overload

- **Definition:** How options are presented (defaults, number of options, order) shapes choices. Choice overload: too many options can reduce choosing or satisfaction.
- **Tag:** Nudges overall: CONTESTED. Choice overload: CONTESTED (average near zero, real under specific conditions).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Mixed field and lab across health, food, finance, environment. Medium transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** Mertens et al. (2022, corrected): d = 0.43 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.48), 447 effect sizes; "structure" interventions (e.g., defaults) strongest. Their own correction: under severe publication bias the effect falls to d = 0.08. Maier et al. (2022) bias-corrected: d = 0.04 (0.00 to 0.14), no evidence overall; structure d = 0.12 (undecided). Choice overload: mean effect "virtually zero" across 63 conditions (Scheibehenne et al. 2010); Chernev et al. (2015, 99 observations) find it does occur when the set is complex, the task difficult, preferences unclear, or the person wants to minimise effort.
- **Carousel use:** A new parent scrolling at night is exactly the high-uncertainty, effort-minimising case, so: one CTA per carousel, the milestone pre-selected to match the carousel topic (an honest default), few visible options. This is cheap insurance even if average effects are small.
- **Manipulative when:** defaults add paid extras, pre-ticked consent for marketing or photo reuse, confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't care about my child's feelings"), or the dark patterns catalogued by Mathur et al. (2019).

**Sources**

- Maier, M., Bartoš, F., Stanley, T. D., Shanks, D. R., Harris, A. J. L., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2022). No evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias. *PNAS, 119*(31), e2200300119. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200300119. Robust Bayesian meta-analysis on Mertens' data. [F]
- Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79*(6), 995-1006. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995. Jam/chocolate/essay studies; 6 options beat 24 to 30. [A]
- Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. *Journal of Consumer Research, 37*(3), 409-425. https://doi.org/10.1086/651235. 50 experiments, N = 5,036. Mean effect near zero, high variance. [A]

## 15. Endowment effect, psychological ownership, IKEA effect, "I designed it myself"

- **Definition:** People value things more once they feel they own them (endowment), feel "it's mine" (psychological ownership), built them (IKEA effect) or designed them (self-design effect).
- **Tag:** Endowment (WTA > WTP): WELL-ESTABLISHED as a pattern, mechanism CONTESTED. Psychological ownership, IKEA and self-design effects: WELL-ESTABLISHED in lab and marketing experiments, replicated conceptually.
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Lab valuation tasks plus mass-customisation toolkits (Franke) and co-creation (Fuchs). Medium transfer risk; mass customisation is close to LumeBook's flow.
- **Effect size:** Not summarised as a single number in the abstracts read. Self-designed products produce "significantly higher willingness to pay", mediated by feelings of accomplishment (Franke et al.). IKEA effect disappears if the creation fails or is destroyed (Norton et al.). Imagery encouraging touch raised perceived ownership even without touching (Peck & Shu).
- **Carousel use:** The free preview is the honest ownership engine: the parent co-creates (photo, name, milestone) and sees a finished-looking book. Carousels can show this process step by step and a parent reacting to their preview. Show the book held in hands at home, which invites imagined touch.
- **Manipulative when:** the preview is held hostage ("your child's book will be deleted in 1 hour"), ownership language is used before purchase to create a false sense of loss, or the child's image is used in remarketing without explicit consent.

**Sources**
- Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1990). Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem. *Journal of Political Economy, 98*(6), 1325-1348. https://doi.org/10.1086/261737. Mug markets; WTA greatly exceeds WTP; undertrading. [A]
- Plott, C. R., & Zeiler, K. (2005). With procedural controls the gap for mugs disappeared. [A]
- Isoni, A., Loomes, G., & Sugden, R. (2011). Comment on Plott & Zeiler. *American Economic Review, 101*(2), 991-1011. https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.2.991. Gap absent for mugs but persistent for lotteries under the same procedures. [A]
- Shu, S. B., & Peck, J. (2011). Psychological ownership and affective reaction: Emotional attachment process variables and the endowment effect. *Journal of Consumer Psychology, 21*(4), 439-452. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.01.002. Nine studies; ownership and affect mediate endowment. [A]

## 16. Mental simulation (imagining using the product)

- **Definition:** Imagining yourself using a product (process simulation) can raise purchase intention, partly via narrative transportation.
- **Tag:** Ad-encouraged process simulation: PARTIAL. "Visual depiction effect" (orient product toward the viewer's dominant hand): CONTESTED (failed high-powered replication).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Ad experiments (Escalas, Elder & Krishna); field sales data for AR try-on (Tan et al.). Medium transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** Elder & Krishna's original purchase-intention effect r = .40 (n = 78) replicated at r = .009 (n = 823, Japan, online) (Motoki & Iseki 2022, who replicated 2 of 10 sensory-marketing findings). AR try-on use associated with higher sales mainly where product uncertainty is high (Tan et al.).
- **Carousel use:** Invite a concrete scene: "tonight, at bedtime, [name] turns the page and sees...". Show the book in a real home moment from the parent's point of view. The free preview is itself a simulation tool that reduces uncertainty, which is where the AR evidence says simulation pays off.
- **Manipulative when:** the simulated outcome is a promise ("imagine: no more tears at kindergarten drop-off").

## 17. Identifiable victim effect

- **Definition:** People give more to help one identified individual than a statistical group.
- **Tag:** CONTESTED.
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Charity donation experiments, often hypothetical. High transfer risk; LumeBook is not a charity appeal.
- **Effect size:** Meta-analytic r = .05 across 41 studies, mainly with a single photographed child in poverty (Lee & Feeley 2016). Reanalysis suggests publication bias; pre-registered replication of Small et al. found ηp² = .000 (Maier et al. 2024, hypothetical donations).
- **Carousel use:** The useful, non-contested part is "one specific child and family" as a narrative device (see principles 4 and 10), not victimhood.

## 18. Emotional salience and virality

- **Definition:** Content that evokes high-arousal emotions (awe, amusement, also anger and anxiety) is shared more; low-arousal sadness is shared less.
- **Tag:** PARTIAL (robust core finding, but which emotions and whether negativity helps varies by platform and audience goal).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Real sharing data (NYT email, video ads across platforms, Upworthy A/B tests). Low transfer risk.
- **Effect size:** Upworthy: each extra negative word in a headline raised CTR by 2.3% (Robertson et al. 2023). But a 2025 preprint across 150,000+ headline tests found no average effect of negative wording, with negativity read as manipulative when readers seek credibility (Banerjee & Urminsky, preprint). Video ads: amusement, excitement, inspiration and warmth increase sharing; information-focused content and early, prominent branding reduce it (Tellis et al. 2019).
- **Carousel use:** Aim for warmth, gentle humour and small awe ("she recognised herself and gasped") rather than anxiety. Keep the logo light until the end.
- **Manipulative when:** anxiety or anger bait aimed at parents ("the mistake that scars kids", "what kindergarten teachers won't tell you").

**Sources**
- Berger, J., & Milkman, K. L. (2012). What makes online content viral? *Journal of Marketing Research, 49*(2), 192-205. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353. NYT articles over 3 months + experiments. Positive more viral than negative; arousal drives sharing. [A]

Numbers above; babies and animals as emotion triggers. [A]
- Robertson, C. E., Pröllochs, N., Schwarzenegger, K., Pärnamets, P., Van Bavel, J. J., & Feuerriegel, S. (2023). ## 19. Faces in social media images

- **Definition:** Posts with human faces attract more attention and interaction.
- **Tag:** PARTIAL (large observational datasets; effect differs by platform and by outcome: likes vs purchase intent).
- **Domain / transfer risk:** Instagram and Twitter data (low transfer risk), but observational.
- **Effect size:** Instagram photos with faces: 38% more likely to get likes, 32% more likely to get comments; number, age and gender of faces did not matter (Bakhshi et al., 1M images). Brand posts: face presence raised engagement on Twitter but not Instagram (Li & Xie 2020). Consumer selfies (faces) got more likes and comments, but "brand selfies" (product held, no face) produced more purchase intent and higher display-ad CTR, via self-reference (Hartmann et al. 2021).
- **Carousel use:** Faces (parent and child reacting, with consent) on hook and peak slides for engagement; a point-of-view "book in my hands" image on the CTA slide, which lets viewers picture themselves holding it.
- **Manipulative when:** using customers' children's faces without explicit consent, or AI-generated "real customer" faces.

**Sources**
- Bakhshi, S., Shamma, D. A., & Gilbert, E. (2014). Faces engage us: Photos with faces attract more likes and comments on Instagram. *Proceedings of CHI 2014*, 965-974. https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557403. 1M Instagram images, controlling for reach and activity. [A]
- Li, Y., & Xie, Y. (2020). Is a picture worth a thousand words? An empirical study of image content and social media engagement. *Journal of Marketing Research, 57*(1), 1-19. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243719881113. Airline and SUV posts on Twitter and Instagram. High-quality professional images help on both; faces and image-text fit help on Twitter only. [A]
- Hartmann, J., Heitmann, M., Schamp, C., & Netzer, O. (2021). The power of brand selfies. *Journal of Marketing Research, 58*(6), 1159-1177. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437211037258. 250,000+ brand-image posts, 185 brands, plus lab experiment. [A]

## Ethics overlay: what the evidence says about guilt, fear and urgency

The brand rules out guilt, fear and false urgency. The evidence shows these tactics can work on average, so the rule is an ethical choice, not a free lunch. It also shows real costs with parents:
- Guilt appeals: small average effect, Hedges' g = 0.19 (26 studies, 7,512 participants; Peng et al. 2023, https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1201631) [A]. With working mothers specifically, moderate guilt ads produced the most felt guilt, while stronger guilt produced anger that lowered attitudes toward the ad and brand (Coulter & Pinto 1995, *Journal of Applied Psychology, 80*(6), 697-705, https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.80.6.697) [A].
- Fear appeals: d = 0.29 across 248 samples (Tannenbaum et al. 2015, *Psychological Bulletin, 141*(6), 1178-1204, https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039729) [A]. Effective, which is exactly why a children's brand has to choose not to use them.
- Scarcity: 416 effect sizes, 131 studies show scarcity cues raise purchase intentions, with type depending on product (Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal 2022, *Journal of Retailing, 98*(4), 741-758, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2022.06.003) [A]. Fake scarcity and countdowns are listed dark patterns (Mathur et al. 2019).
- Persuasion knowledge: once people recognise a tactic as a tactic, they reinterpret the message and the marketer (Friestad & Wright 1994, *Journal of Consumer Research, 21*(1), 1-31, https://doi.org/10.1086/209380) [A]. Parents of young children see a lot of marketing; heavy tactics are likely to be recognised.

# Part 2. Product-specific evidence

## A. Personalised children's books

**What exists (all verified records):**
- Kucirkova, N., Messer, D., & Whitelock, D. (2013, online 2012). Parents reading with their toddlers: The role of personalization in book engagement. *Journal of Early Childhood Literacy, 13*(4), 445-470. https://doi.org/10.1177/1468798412438068. 7 parent-child dyads, children 12 to 33 months, at home; personalised vs non-personalised vs favourite book. More smiles and laughs with the personalised book; more vocal activity than with the favourite book. [A]
- Kucirkova, N., Messer, D., & Sheehy, K. (2014). Reading personalized books with preschool children enhances their word acquisition. *First Language, 34*(3), 227-243. https://doi.org/10.1177/0142723714534221. 18 children (mean age 3;10), within-subject, one book with personalised and generic sections, read twice a week apart. Better knowledge of new words placed in personalised sections. [A]
- Kucirkova, N., Messer, D., & Sheehy, K. (2014). The effects of personalisation on young children's spontaneous speech during shared book reading. *Journal of Pragmatics, 71*, 45-55. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2014.07.007. 35 children (about 37 months). Longer reading time, more self-references, more story-related questions and utterances in personalised sections. [S: abstract summary via search; publisher page blocked]
- Kucirkova, N., Gattis, M., Spargo, T., Seisdedos de Vega, B., & Flewitt, R. (2021). An empirical investigation of parent-child shared reading of digital personalized books. *International Journal of Educational Research, 105*, 101710. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijer.2020.101710. 26 British mothers and 3 to 4 year olds, lab, iPad book with name, photo, favourite toy and food on some pages. **No difference** in vocabulary learning between personalised and generic pages (learning "very low in both"), **no difference** in amount of mothers' talk or child engagement; children's talk focused on self; authors stress the parent's role in moving beyond the self. [F]
- Neuenschwander, R., Brüniger, P., & Fellmann, C. (2025). Personalization effects on child involvement during shared book reading: Do children of color benefit most? *European Journal of Psychology of Education, 40*(4). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10212-025-01026-5. 80 kindergarten children, block-randomised to a personalised (parent-configured name and appearance incl. skin colour) or traditional copy, read individually. Personalisation improved story retelling, text comprehension, and verbal and behavioural involvement; involvement gains were larger for children of colour. [A]
- Dulay, K. M., Masento, N., Harvey, K., Messer, D., & Houston-Price, C. (2020). Me and my veggies: The use of interactive, personalised picture books in healthy eating interventions. *Nutrition Bulletin, 45*(1), 51-58. https://doi.org/10.1111/nbu.12415. Proposal of mechanisms, not an outcome study. [A]
- Related: Cunningham et al. (2014) self-reference memory advantage in 4 to 6 year olds (see principle 9). [A]

**What can honestly be said**
- "Small studies have found that young children are more engaged, smile and talk more when they appear in the story."
- "Some studies found children remembered new words or the story better when it was personalised; others found no difference."
- "Seeing themselves as the hero is something many children enjoy" (supported by engagement data; keep it as enjoyment, not development).

**What cannot be said**
- "Research proves personalised books help children cope with change / reduce anxiety / prepare for a sibling or school." No study tested this.
- "Personalised books improve language development / emotional intelligence." Evidence is small-sample, short-term, mixed, and mostly from one research group.
- Anything about AI-generated look-alike illustrations: no study tests photo-based AI likeness specifically. The studies used names, photos or simple appearance choices.
- Caveats to keep in mind: samples of 7 to 80 children, UK and Switzerland, mostly lab or researcher-read sessions; children may focus on themselves rather than the story, so the parent's conversation matters (Kucirkova et al. 2021).

## B. Social Stories (Carol Gray)

**What exists**
- Kokina, A., & Kern, L. (2010). Social Story interventions for students with autism spectrum disorders: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 40*(7), 812-826. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-009-0931-0. Single-subject studies. "Low to questionable overall effectiveness"; better for reducing inappropriate behaviours than teaching social skills. [A]
- Wright, B., Blackwell, J. E., Bell, K. J., Teige, C., Mandefield, L., Wang, H. I., et al. (2025). Autism Spectrum Social Stories in Schools Trial 2 (ASSSIST-2): A pragmatic randomised controlled trial. *Child and Adolescent Mental Health, 30*(1), 4-12. https://doi.org/10.1111/camh.12740. 87 UK schools, 249 autistic children aged 4 to 11. Primary outcome (SRS-2) not significant (-1.61, 95% CI -4.18 to 0.96); individual goals met more often; no differences in anxiety, depression, parental stress. [A]
- Smith, E., Constantin, A., Johnson, H., & Brosnan, M. (2021). Digitally-mediated Social Stories support children on the autism spectrum adapting to a change in a "real-world" context. *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 51*(2), 514-526. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-020-04558-5. 10 children, summer camp, pre-post teacher ratings without a control group: better understanding, lower anxiety. Practitioner survey (103) says Social Stories are often used for change. [A]
- Zhou, N., Zhou, L., Ho, C. Y. T., McGrath, C., & Wong, H. M. (2024). Social story intervention for training expected behaviors among preschool children: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *IJERPH, 21*(7), 940. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph21070940. 21 studies of children aged 2 to 6, including typically developing children. Most reported improvements; only 2 studies could be pooled (more toothbrushing steps, MD = 0.66); calls for better RCTs. [A]
- Wahman, C. L., Pustejovsky, J. E., Ostrosky, M. M., & Santos, R. M. (2019 preprint). Examining the effects of social stories on challenging behavior and prosocial skills in young children: A systematic review and meta-analysis. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/fch6t. 12 single-case studies, 30 children aged 3 to 5; variable rigor and efficacy. [A, preprint]
- Guo, T., et al. (2026). The effects of social story interventions on individuals with ASD: A meta-analysis of single-case experimental studies. *Frontiers in Psychology, 17*, 1855311. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1855311. 21 studies, 61 participants; Tau-U = 0.743 (single-case metric, not comparable to d). [A]

**Assessment:** Evidence is mostly autism, mostly single-case designs, with the best-controlled trial (ASSSIST-2) null on its primary outcome. For typically developing children there are scattered behaviour-training studies (toothbrushing, routines) and no evidence on milestone transitions like a new sibling. "Social Stories" is also a trademarked method with specific authoring criteria.

**Safe wording:** "A story that walks your child through what is about to happen, in simple, positive steps." Avoid "Social Story", "therapeutic", "clinically proven", "recommended for autism", and any implication that LumeBook is an intervention.

## C. Bibliotherapy and stories for transitions

**What exists**
- Rapee, R. M., Abbott, M. J., & Lyneham, H. J. (2006). Bibliotherapy for children with anxiety disorders using written materials for parents: A randomized controlled trial. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 74*(3), 436-444. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.74.3.436. 267 clinically anxious children aged 6 to 12. Parent-delivered CBT from written materials beat waitlist (about 15% more children diagnosis-free at 12 and 24 weeks) but was less effective than therapist group treatment. [A]
- Yuan, S., et al. (2018). Comparative efficacy and acceptability of bibliotherapy for depression and anxiety disorders in children and adolescents: A meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials. *Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment, 14*, 353-365. https://doi.org/10.2147/NDT.S152747. 8 RCTs, 979 participants, SMD = -0.52 vs controls. Bibliotherapy here means treatment delivered through written self-help materials for diagnosed or elevated symptoms, not reading picture books for enjoyment. [A]
- Montgomery, P., & Maunders, K. (2015). The effectiveness of creative bibliotherapy for internalizing, externalizing, and prosocial behaviors in children: A systematic review. *Children and Youth Services Review, 55*, 37-47. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.childyouth.2015.05.010. 8 RCTs, ages 5 to 16, fiction-based. "Small to moderate positive effect"; too heterogeneous to pool. [A, via University of Birmingham repository]
- Redman, H., Mutanda, D., Bethel, A., Miller, R., Meléndez-Torres, G. J., & Green, J. (2025). Does creative bibliotherapy delivered in schools improve mental health-related outcomes for 5-16 year olds? A systematic review. *Arts & Health, 18*(2), 164-186. https://doi.org/10.1080/17533015.2025.2599866. 13 of the included studies (57%) reported positive impact; few were well defended against bias; "promising indications... but, to date, no strong evidence". [A]
- Volling, B. L. (2012). Family transitions following the birth of a sibling: An empirical review of changes in the firstborn's adjustment. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(3), 497-528. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026921. 30 studies. No support for a "crisis" model; large individual differences. Notes that sibling-preparation classes tell children stories about being a big brother or sister, but that recommendations need well-designed longitudinal studies. [F, searched full text]
- Cutler, L., & Slicker, G. (2020). Picture book portrayals of the transition to kindergarten: Who is responsible? *Early Childhood Education Journal, 48*(6), 793-813. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10643-020-01040-w. Content analysis of 52 picture books about starting kindergarten (no child outcomes). [M; sample size from search snippet, S]
- Shechtman, Z. (University of Haifa) has small studies of affective bibliotherapy in therapy groups, e.g., Shechtman (1999), *Child Psychiatry and Human Development*, https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022671009144 (5 aggressive boys, single-subject design). [A]

**Key distinction:** Parent-led CBT bibliotherapy (Rapee) is a structured treatment programme with exercises for parents of children with diagnosed anxiety. Creative bibliotherapy uses fiction but usually with a trained facilitator and discussion. A personalised picture book read at home is neither. Effects from those programmes cannot be transferred to LumeBook.

**Safe wording:** "Many parents use stories to talk about big changes." "A gentle way to start the conversation about the new baby." Avoid "reduces anxiety", "prepares your child emotionally", "bibliotherapy", "eases the transition" stated as a fact.

## D. Shared reading and parent-child conversation

**What exists**
- Whitehurst, G. J., Falco, F. L., Lonigan, C. J., Fischel, J. E., DeBaryshe, B. D., Valdez-Menchaca, M. C., & Caulfield, M. (1988). Accelerating language development through picture book reading. *Developmental Psychology, 24*(4), 552-559. https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.24.4.552. Origin of dialogic reading. [M]
- Mol, S. E., Bus, A. G., de Jong, M. T., & Smeets, D. J. H. (2008). Added value of dialogic parent-child book readings: A meta-analysis. *Early Education and Development, 19*(1), 7-26. https://doi.org/10.1080/10409280701838603. 16 studies; expressive vocabulary d = 0.59; smaller for 4 to 5 year olds and at-risk children. [A]
- Noble, C., Sala, G., Peter, M., Lingwood, J., Rowland, C., Gobet, F., & Pine, J. (2019). The impact of shared book reading on children's language skills: A meta-analysis. *Educational Research Review, 28*, 100290. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2019.100290. g = 0.194 overall; negligible (g = 0.028) against active controls. [A]
- Dowdall, N., Melendez-Torres, G. J., Murray, L., Gardner, F., Hartford, L., & Cooper, P. J. (2020). Shared picture book reading interventions for child language development: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Child Development, 91*(2), e383-e399. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13225. 19 RCTs, N = 2,594: expressive d = 0.41, receptive d = 0.26, caregiver book-sharing competence d = 1.01; low dosage gives minimal effect. [A]
- Tompkins, V., Benigno, J. P., Kiger Lee, B., & Wright, B. M. (2018). The relation between parents' mental state talk and children's social understanding: A meta-analysis. *Social Development, 27*(2), 223-246. https://doi.org/10.1111/sode.12280. 22 + 18 studies, 2,298 children under 7. Parents' talk about thoughts and feelings relates to children's false-belief and emotion understanding; for false belief, links were stronger in book contexts than in reminiscing. Correlational. [A]
- Aram, D., Fine, Y., & Ziv, M. (2013). Enhancing parent-child shared book reading interactions: Promoting references to the book's plot and socio-cognitive themes. *Early Childhood Research Quarterly, 28*(1), 111-122. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecresq.2012.03.005. Israeli study, 58 low-SES families. Parents guided to discuss mental states, causes and links to the child's life did so more, and so did their children, than controls who only got the books. [A, via ERIC]
- Schapira, R., & Grazzani, I. (2025). Shared book reading and promoting social and emotional competences in educational settings: A narrative review. *Frontiers in Psychology, 16*, 1622536. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1622536. 15 studies in nursery and preschool settings. [A]

**What this supports:** Books are a natural, well-studied context for parent-child talk about feelings, and simple guidance increases that talk (Aram et al.). That supports "a book can help start a conversation", and supports including 2 to 3 suggested conversation prompts at the end of each LumeBook story or carousel.

**What it does not support:** That reading a LumeBook will improve language, emotion understanding or social skills. Language effects shrink with stronger controls (Noble et al.) and depend on dosage and adult technique (Dowdall et al.), and the social-understanding evidence is correlational.

# Part 4. Gaps and items not fully verified

- Pooled effect sizes for the segmenting, signalling and seductive-details meta-analyses (Rey 2012; Rey et al. 2019; Richter et al. 2016; Mayer 2024) could not be opened; they are listed as [M] or [S].
- Bornstein (1989) pooled mere-exposure effect and Wilson & Sherrell (1993) numbers not retrieved.
- Banerjee & Urminsky (2025) specific finding that question headlines lower click-through came from a search summary, not the paper text.
- Scheibehenne et al. (2016) result and the 2017 exchange were not read beyond metadata.
- No peer-reviewed study was found on Instagram carousels specifically (swipe depth, slide count) or on AI-generated look-alike children's books.
- No study was found testing whether reading a story about a new sibling, pacifier weaning, potty training or starting school changes children's adjustment. Volling (2012) explicitly calls for such studies.
