## Development sources

- [Milestones by 4 Years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html) · CDC, Learn the Signs. Act Early. · read 2026-10-13
  - supports: 4 year language milestones (sentences of four or more words; answers simple questions about what things are for) plus the tip to play simple games and matching games. Read today in the in-app browser (plain curl gets 403); sentences joined with ' / ' in page order (milestone list, then the tips list). The page does not say board games build vocabulary; it lists games only as a general tip. Milestones are what 75 percent or more of children do by an age.
- [Milestones by 5 Years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html) · CDC, Learn the Signs. Act Early. · read 2026-10-13
  - supports: 5 year conversation milestone and the tips to ask 'Why?' and 'How?' while a child plays and to play card games. Read today in the in-app browser; sentences joined with ' / ' in page order. The tips are general parenting tips, not about these two products, and the page does not mention board games for vocabulary specifically. Also: By 5 most children use or recognise simple rhymes, tell a story with two events and answer simple questions about a book; CDC also lists naming some letters (cognitive section) and suggests rhyming games and asking questions while reading. The milestones describe what most children do, not a deadline. Read through a fetch tool; the quotes are the page's words as the tool returned them (the 'Writes some letters in her name' and 'Names some letters' milestones were reported under Cognitive).
- [Language Development in Your 4- to 5-Year-Old](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/comm-4-to-5.html) · Nemours KidsHealth (medically reviewed by Mary L. Gavin, MD, May 2022) · read 2026-10-13
  - supports: Names 'playing word games' and 'asking questions' among everyday ways to support vocabulary at 4 to 5. A general parent explainer, not research on board games; its own wording is 'will help', so our copy must soften it to 'a chance to practise' (no claim that a game causes vocabulary growth).
- [Story Time for Preschoolers](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/reading-preschool.html) · Nemours KidsHealth · read 2026-10-13
  - supports: Supports using rhymes and poems, alphabet books, pointing out letters or words, and letting the child retell the story as playful reading practice at preschool age. It is general parent guidance, not a test of any specific book.
- [Is Your Child Ready to Read?](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-to-Read.aspx) · HealthyChildren.org, American Academy of Pediatrics · read 2026-10-13
  - supports: Supports a relaxed, child-led tone: interest in letters and listening at story time are signs of readiness, and drilling is discouraged. Used for the caveat that children differ in pace, not for any claim about a book. The quotes are from the fetch tool's extraction of the page.
- [Nursery Rhyme Knowledge and Phonological Awareness in Preschool Children](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1097164) · Journal of Language and Literacy Education, v7 n1 (via ERIC, Institute of Education Sciences), L. J. Harper, 2011 · read 2026-10-13
  - supports: A peer-reviewed study of pre-K children and nursery rhyme instruction; the abstract (all I read, not the full paper) calls phonological awareness an important precursor to learning to read. Small study with English nursery rhymes, so it does not show that any Hebrew book causes a skill; use only for 'rhymes and sound play are an opportunity to practise listening to sounds'.
