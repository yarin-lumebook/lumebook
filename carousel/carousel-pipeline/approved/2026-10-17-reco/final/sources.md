## Development sources

- [Milestones by 5 Years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html) · CDC, Learn the Signs. Act Early. · read 2026-10-16
  - supports: CDC suggests, for age 5, playing with puzzles and building blocks, simple board and card games with rules, and asking 'Why?' and 'How?'. Read today through WebFetch (CDC blocks plain curl); sentences joined with ' / ', and the tool returned them as extracted text, not a raw HTML copy. Limits: these are parent tips for age 5, not a milestone about problem solving, and the page does not mention thinking ahead or trying another way. The 6-years milestone page returned 404, so CDC covers only up to 5 here. Use for 'around 5, puzzles and games with rules are everyday ways to practise', never for a promised outcome.
- [7 Year Well-Child Checkup](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/checkup-7yrs.html) · Nemours KidsHealth (medically reviewed by Elana Pearl Ben-Joseph, MD, August 2024) · read 2026-10-16
  - supports: Nemours KidsHealth lists, among common development at 7, longer attention spans and solving problems in a more organized and logical way. Read today by curl; the list items are joined with ' ... ' and ' / ' (the page shows them as bullets). Limits: a general checkup page for parents, 'common for many kids' not a test; it says nothing about games, puzzles, planning ahead or persistence. It supports the general direction at about age 7, not the 5 to 6 end of the range.
- [Can executive functions be improved by playing board games across the lifespan? A systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42532119/) · Estrada-Plana et al., Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 2026;18(4):e70194, doi:10.1111/aphw.70194 (PubMed, NIH) · read 2026-10-16
  - supports: Peer reviewed review of 24 studies (16 in the meta-analysis): promising but NOT sufficient for a strong recommendation that board games improve executive functions. Effects were in short-term memory (verbal in children; visuospatial in kindergarten), not planning or problem solving as such, and kindergarten is not ages 5 to 7 exactly. Only the abstract was read (PubMed page shows a cookie notice, so the abstract came from NCBI's efetch service for PMID 42532119). Use only as a soft 'research suggests board games are a promising way to exercise thinking skills', never 'prove' or 'improve'.
- [Just Play Cognitive Modern Board and Card Games, It's Going to Be Good for Your Executive Functions: A Randomized Controlled Trial with Children at Risk of Social Exclusion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37761453/) · Moya-Higueras et al., Children (Basel) 2023;10(9):1492, doi:10.3390/children10091492 (PubMed, NIH) · read 2026-10-16
  - supports: Randomized trial, 68 children aged 7 to 12 at risk of social exclusion: cognitive flexibility and inhibition improved over time in both groups that played modern board and card games. Limits: ages 7 to 12 (only the top of our range), a vulnerable population, no non-playing control group in the abstract, and it measured flexibility, inhibition and memory, not planning or problem solving. Only the abstract was read (NCBI efetch for PMID 37761453); sentences joined with ' ... '. Use for 'a chance to practise flexible thinking', never a promised result.
