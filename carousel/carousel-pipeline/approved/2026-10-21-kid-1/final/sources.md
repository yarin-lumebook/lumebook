  - quote: "Most brothers and sisters have some degree of jealousy or competition, which can lead to arguments and bickering. ... When kids argue, if possible, don't get involved. Step in only if there's a danger of physical harm. If you always intervene, you risk creating other problems. The kids may start expecting your help and wait for you to come to the rescue rather than learning to work out the problems on their own. ... Separate kids until they're calm. Sometimes it's best just to give them space for a little while and not rehash the conflict. ... Don't put too much focus on figuring out which child is to blame. It takes two to fight — anyone who is involved is partly responsible. ... try to set up a "win–win" situation so that each child gains something. When they both want the same toy, perhaps there's a game they could play together instead. ... as kids cope with conflict, they also learn important skills that will serve them for life — like how to value another person's point of view, how to compromise and negotiate, and how to control aggressive impulses. ... Set ground rules for acceptable behavior. Tell the kids to keep their hands to themselves ... Get their input on the rules ... Don't let kids make you think that everything always has to be "fair" and "equal" — sometimes one kid needs more than the other. ... Give your kids one-on-one attention directed to their interests and needs. ... make sure kids have their own space and time to do their own thing ... Many kids fight over their caregiver's attention, so fun family activities can help reduce conflict. ... If you're concerned by the language used or name-calling, it's OK to "coach" kids through what they're feeling by using appropriate words. ... School-age kids often have a strong concept of fairness, so might not understand why siblings of other ages are treated differently"
- [Sibling Relationships: How to Help Your Kids Build Healthy Bonds](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/Pages/sibling-synergy.aspx) · HealthyChildren.org, American Academy of Pediatrics (last updated 6 Apr 2026) · read 2026-10-16
  - supports: Fights over a favourite toy are common among younger siblings; teamwork-based games or not keeping score can help; disagreeing respectfully builds communication and coping skills; younger siblings need more intervention and older ones less; keep ground rules clear; give a simple disagreement a chance to be solved by the children; listen fairly before deciding; one-on-one time with each child, for example a nightly chat at bedtime.   - quote: "A younger child, for example, may get upset when their sibling wants a favorite toy. ... If your kids struggle with sibling rivalry, pick teamwork-based games or don't keep score. ... Learning to disagree respectfully is part of developing effective communication and building coping skills. ... With younger siblings, you will need to intervene more often. ... As your children get older and become more mature, you can lessen your involvement. ... Always make sure your family's ground rules are clear. ... If it is a simple disagreement, give them a chance to solve it on their own. ... Do your best to listen fairly before making any decisions. ... While time together as a family is important, one-on-one time with each child is important too. ... Try fitting it into your daily routines, such as during a nightly chat at bedtime."
- [When Siblings Won't Stop Fighting](https://childmind.org/article/when-siblings-wont-stop-fighting/) · Child Mind Institute (writer Katherine Martinelli, clinical expert reviewer Stephanie A. Lee, PsyD; last reviewed 5 Feb 2025) · read 2026-10-16
  - supports: Look for the pattern and the root cause rather than only breaking up each fight: the fight is often not about the toy, and kids can learn that yelling or kicking gets a parent involved at once; praise cooperation as it happens (aim for three to five positive moments for every fight); make rules and outcomes clear ahead of time; if a toy is taken away give it back within minutes.
