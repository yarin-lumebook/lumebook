# Sources · expert-1 · 2026-10-30

- [Nightmares, Night Terrors & Sleepwalking in Children: How Parents Can Help](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Nightmares-and-Night-Terrors.aspx) · HealthyChildren.org, American Academy of Pediatrics (last updated 21 Sep 2023) · read 2026-10-24
  - supports: Go quickly; assure the child you are there; remind that dreams are not real; allow a light; check for shadows that scare; encourage sleep once ready.
  - quote: "Nightmares are scary dreams that often happen during the second half of the night when dreaming is most intense. ... They tend to peak between 3 and 12 years old. ... If your child has a bad dream, the best response is to comfort them. ... Go to your child as quickly as you can. Assure them that you are there and will not let anything harm them. Encourage them to tell you what happened in the dream. Remind them that dreams are not real. Allow them to keep a light on if it makes them feel better. Once your child is ready, encourage them to go back to sleep. See if there is something that is scaring your child, like shadows. If so, make sure they are gone."
- [Nightmares](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/nightmare.html) · Nemours KidsHealth (medically reviewed by Amy W. Anzilotti, MD, June 2023) · read 2026-10-24
  - supports: Calm presence; label it as a bad dream that is over; talk only briefly at night; in the morning tell, draw or write about it and think up a new ending; reading is part of a calming routine; see the doctor if more than once a week, if sleep suffers or with other troubles.
  - quote: "Reassure your child that you're there. Your calm presence helps your child feel safe and protected after waking up feeling afraid. ... Label what's happened. Let your child know that it was a nightmare and now it's over. You might say something like, "You had a bad dream, but now you're awake and everything is OK." ... No need to talk more than briefly about the nightmare in the wee hours ... But in the morning, your child may want to tell you all about last night's scary dream. Talking about it — maybe even drawing the dream or writing about it — in the daylight can help scary images lose their power. Your child might enjoy thinking up a new (more satisfying) ending to the scary dream. ... This might include a bath, a snuggle from you, reading, or some quiet talk about the pleasant events of the day. ... Talk to the doctor if your child's nightmares happen more than once a week, often prevent your child from getting enough sleep, or happen along with other emotional or behavioral troubles."
- [Nightmares](https://sleepeducation.org/sleep-disorders/nightmares/) · AASM Sleep Education, American Academy of Sleep Medicine (October 2020, reviewed by Reeba Mathew, MD and Anne M. Morse, DO) · read 2026-10-24
  - supports: Nightmares are especially typical at ages 3 to 6; respond quickly; talk to the pediatrician if recurring nightmares greatly disturb the child.
  - quote: "Nightmares are especially typical in children aged 3-6 years. Up to 50% of young children have severe nightmares that cause them to wake up their parents. ... Respond quickly to comfort your child when he or she wakes up from a nightmare. ... Talk to your child's pediatrician if recurring nightmares greatly disturb your child."
