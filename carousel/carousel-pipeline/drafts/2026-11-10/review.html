      Dummies · Better Health Channel, Victorian Department of Health (reviewed 12 Jun 2026) · נקרא 2026-10-29

      Most children stop sucking habits between the ages of two and four. Avoid trying to stop dummy use suddenly, as it can lead to other oral habits such as thumb or finger sucking. Limit the use of the dummy to sleep or nighttime. Consider replacing it with a toy, maybe a cuddly toy they have chosen and can hold when they would be using the dummy. Give plenty of encouragement and keep it positive. The first few days without a dummy are likely to be the most difficult.

      Stopping use of a dummy and/or bottle · Derbyshire Family Health Service (NHS) · נקרא 2026-10-29

      Help them learn new ways to soothe themselves, such as using a favourite blanket or toy instead of the dummy. Try sending the dummy away to Santa, the Easter Bunny, the Tooth Fairy or the Dummy Fairy. Praise times without the dummy. Keep it out of sight and reach. Parents and carers, including childcare settings, should follow the same steps so the message is consistent. Try to remove the dummy once they are asleep.

      Weaning off dummies and bottles · Sheffield Children's NHS Foundation Trust, Resource Library · נקרא 2026-10-29

      Start by just letting your child use their dummy for nap times and bed time. Once they are using their dummy less, set a date for being 'dummy free'. Make sure you and other caregivers remove any bottles and dummies from your child's sight. Using distraction techniques can help when asked for bottles or dummies.

              Potty Training: 12 Tips to Keep the Process Positive · HealthyChildren.org, American Academy of Pediatrics (last updated 6 Nov 2023) · נקרא 2026-10-29

              Pick a potty-chair. Children's feet should be able to reach the floor. Be a role model. Let your children see you use the toilet and wash your hands afterward. Choose positive words your family will use to describe body parts, urine and bowel movements. Make a habit of seating your children onto the potty-chair first thing in the morning. When toilet training starts, switch to big-kid underwear. Avoid a power struggle. Remember that children control when and where they pee and poop. Understand their fear. To give your children a feeling of control, let them flush the toilet.
