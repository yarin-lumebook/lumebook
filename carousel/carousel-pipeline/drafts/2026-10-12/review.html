Activity changes for kids: behaviour tips · Raising Children Network (Australia; last updated or reviewed 01-10-2024) · נקרא 2026-10-11

Children can accept change better when they know it’s coming. ... Give your child some warning about activity changes that are coming up. For example, ‘Desi, you have 5 more minutes to play. Then it will be time to go home’, or ‘Desi, one more go on the slide and then we’re going home’. ... Avoid giving your child a choice about an activity change if there isn’t really a choice. For example, when you say, ‘Orla, would you like to pack up those toys now?’ you suggest a choice. Instead you could say, ‘Orla, please start packing up those toys now’. ... Link something your child doesn’t want to do with something that they like. For example, ‘First we clean up the toys, and then we have a snack’. ... Choose your timing. If you can, stop one thing and start another during a natural break in your child’s activity. ... Give your child a choice about things that are part of the activity change. For example, ‘Evan, we have to go in the car in a minute. You can take one toy with you. Which one will it be?’ ... Limit options. For example, let your child choose between 2 different t-shirts, but not all the t-shirts in the drawer. ... Try making activity change fun. For example, ‘Can you march like a soldier to the car?’ ... Point out good things your child can look forward to after the change. ... It’s natural for your child to be disappointed about having to stop an activity that they’re enjoying. ... ‘I know you feel frustrated that you didn’t have time to play another game’. ... Role-play changes in activities, and talk with your child about how they might go.

Activity changes for kids: behaviour tips · Raising Children Network (Australia; last updated or reviewed 01-10-2024) · נקרא 2026-10-11

Children can accept change better when they know it’s coming. ... Give your child some warning about activity changes that are coming up. For example, ‘Desi, you have 5 more minutes to play. Then it will be time to go home’, or ‘Desi, one more go on the slide and then we’re going home’. ... Avoid giving your child a choice about an activity change if there isn’t really a choice. For example, when you say, ‘Orla, would you like to pack up those toys now?’ you suggest a choice. Instead you could say, ‘Orla, please start packing up those toys now’. ... Link something your child doesn’t want to do with something that they like. For example, ‘First we clean up the toys, and then we have a snack’. ... Choose your timing. If you can, stop one thing and start another during a natural break in your child’s activity. ... Give your child a choice about things that are part of the activity change. For example, ‘Evan, we have to go in the car in a minute. You can take one toy with you. Which one will it be?’ ... Limit options. For example, let your child choose between 2 different t-shirts, but not all the t-shirts in the drawer. ... Try making activity change fun. For example, ‘Can you march like a soldier to the car?’ ... Point out good things your child can look forward to after the change. ... It’s natural for your child to be disappointed about having to stop an activity that they’re enjoying. ... ‘I know you feel frustrated that you didn’t have time to play another game’. ... Role-play changes in activities, and talk with your child about how they might go.

Children can accept change better when they know it’s coming. ... Give your child some warning about activity changes that are coming up. For example, ‘Desi, you have 5 more minutes to play. Then it will be time to go home’, or ‘Desi, one more go on the slide and then we’re going home’. ... Avoid giving your child a choice about an activity change if there isn’t really a choice. For example, when you say, ‘Orla, would you like to pack up those toys now?’ you suggest a choice. Instead you could say, ‘Orla, please start packing up those toys now’. ... Link something your child doesn’t want to do with something that they like. For example, ‘First we clean up the toys, and then we have a snack’. ... Choose your timing. If you can, stop one thing and start another during a natural break in your child’s activity. ... Give your child a choice about things that are part of the activity change. For example, ‘Evan, we have to go in the car in a minute. You can take one toy with you. Which one will it be?’ ... Limit options. For example, let your child choose between 2 different t-shirts, but not all the t-shirts in the drawer. ... Try making activity change fun. For example, ‘Can you march like a soldier to the car?’ ... Point out good things your child can look forward to after the change. ... Activity changes for kids: behaviour tips · Raising Children Network (Australia; last updated or reviewed 01-10-2024) · נקרא 2026-10-11

Children can accept change better when they know it’s coming. ... Give your child some warning about activity changes that are coming up. For example, ‘Desi, you have 5 more minutes to play. Then it will be time to go home’, or ‘Desi, one more go on the slide and then we’re going home’. ... Avoid giving your child a choice about an activity change if there isn’t really a choice. For example, when you say, ‘Orla, would you like to pack up those toys now?’ you suggest a choice. Instead you could say, ‘Orla, please start packing up those toys now’. ... Link something your child doesn’t want to do with something that they like. For example, ‘First we clean up the toys, and then we have a snack’. ... Choose your timing. If you can, stop one thing and start another during a natural break in your child’s activity. ... Give your child a choice about things that are part of the activity change. For example, ‘Evan, we have to go in the car in a minute. You can take one toy with you. Which one will it be?’ ... Limit options. For example, let your child choose between 2 different t-shirts, but not all the t-shirts in the drawer. ... Try making activity change fun. For example, ‘Can you march like a soldier to the car?’ ... Point out good things your child can look forward to after the change. ... It’s natural for your child to be disappointed about having to stop an activity that they’re enjoying. ... ‘I know you feel frustrated that you didn’t have time to play another game’. ... Role-play changes in activities, and talk with your child about how they might go.

Activity changes for kids: behaviour tips · Raising Children Network (Australia; last updated or reviewed 01-10-2024) · נקרא 2026-10-11

Children can accept change better when they know it’s coming. ... Give your child some warning about activity changes that are coming up. For example, ‘Desi, you have 5 more minutes to play. Then it will be time to go home’, or ‘Desi, one more go on the slide and then we’re going home’. ... Avoid giving your child a choice about an activity change if there isn’t really a choice. For example, when you say, ‘Orla, would you like to pack up those toys now?’ you suggest a choice. Instead you could say, ‘Orla, please start packing up those toys now’. ... Link something your child doesn’t want to do with something that they like. For example, ‘First we clean up the toys, and then we have a snack’. ... Choose your timing. If you can, stop one thing and start another during a natural break in your child’s activity. ... Give your child a choice about things that are part of the activity change. For example, ‘Evan, we have to go in the car in a minute. You can take one toy with you. Which one will it be?’ ... Limit options. For example, let your child choose between 2 different t-shirts, but not all the t-shirts in the drawer. ... Try making activity change fun. For example, ‘Can you march like a soldier to the car?’ ... Point out good things your child can look forward to after the change. ... It’s natural for your child to be disappointed about having to stop an activity that they’re enjoying. ... ‘I know you feel frustrated that you didn’t have time to play another game’. ... Role-play changes in activities, and talk with your child about how they might go.

Activity changes for kids: behaviour tips · Raising Children Network (Australia; last updated or reviewed 01-10-2024) · נקרא 2026-10-11

Children can accept change better when they know it’s coming. ... Give your child some warning about activity changes that are coming up. For example, ‘Desi, you have 5 more minutes to play. Then it will be time to go home’, or ‘Desi, one more go on the slide and then we’re going home’. ... Avoid giving your child a choice about an activity change if there isn’t really a choice. For example, when you say, ‘Orla, would you like to pack up those toys now?’ you suggest a choice. Instead you could say, ‘Orla, please start packing up those toys now’. ... Link something your child doesn’t want to do with something that they like. For example, ‘First we clean up the toys, and then we have a snack’. ... Choose your timing. If you can, stop one thing and start another during a natural break in your child’s activity. ... Give your child a choice about things that are part of the activity change. For example, ‘Evan, we have to go in the car in a minute. You can take one toy with you. Which one will it be?’ ... Limit options. For example, let your child choose between 2 different t-shirts, but not all the t-shirts in the drawer. ... Try making activity change fun. For example, ‘Can you march like a soldier to the car?’ ... Point out good things your child can look forward to after the change. ... It’s natural for your child to be disappointed about having to stop an activity that they’re enjoying. ... ‘I know you feel frustrated that you didn’t have time to play another game’. ... Role-play changes in activities, and talk with your child about how they might go.
