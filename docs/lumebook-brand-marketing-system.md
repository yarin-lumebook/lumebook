## 1. The decision at the centre

Yarin's workshop direction: three buyer routes (gift buyer, parent around a developmental event, kindergarten group gift); imaginative, magical brand expression that brings the child's dreams and fantasies into the story. Source: Yarin's answers in this chat, 13 October 2026, recorded here. These are strategic inputs, not research findings about customers.

Recommended emotional territory to explore following that input: **possibility**, expressed through a child picturing themselves as the story's hero. This is an unverified creative hypothesis. Keep personal recognition as a comparison, rather than treating the previous draft as a settled decision.

Child-facing creative lens to test: “That could be me.” Personal-recognition alternative: “You see me. You know me. I matter to you.”

Buyer-facing hypotheses: gift buyer, “I can give them that experience”; parent preparing for a change, “I have a way to explore this with them”; kindergarten buyer, “I can give each child a personal part of our shared occasion.” These proposed motivations need validation.

Use one coherent brand experience while allowing each buying route its own motivation. Compare possibility, delight at becoming the hero, and personal recognition within a single occasion. Explore parent reassurance in the transition route separately. Yarin's subsequent instruction asks for an aggressive parental-guilt direction; the scoped experiment below supersedes the previous no-guilt recommendation for that direction. Buying a book is not evidence of good parenting.

### Parent guilt experiment, requested by Yarin

Latest workshop input, 13 October 2026: Yarin explicitly wants to explore parental guilt in a highly confrontational way. This is a creative brief, not evidence that guilt improves sales or that the audience experiences a particular failure.

Proposed tension to test: the gap between the attention a parent intends to give and the attention they make time for. Hypothetical inner sentence: “I keep saying these moments matter, then I keep putting them off.” Desired next step: make time for a shared story; the personalised book is an optional way to do that, not a purchase that erases guilt.

Candidate fictional creative setups, not customer observations: a bedtime story postponed, a phone taking attention during an invitation to read, or preparation for a new sibling repeatedly delayed. Start with the postponed story because the action and the product use can be shown together. This selection is a recommendation, not a proven performance ranking.

English hook candidates to test:

- “How many times did ‘later’ win today?”
- “What got your attention tonight? Your child, or your phone?”
- “Is the story you promised still waiting?”

Proposed product bridge: “Preview a story starring your child. Make time to read it together.” Verify the actual preview and product before publishing. Do not claim personalisation causes connection, prevents emotional harm or repairs a relationship without evidence.

Intensity boundary: confront a specific choice or postponed action. Do not invent child suffering, present imagined child thoughts as testimony, or claim that not buying harms the child. Do not describe guilt-based message effectiveness as established.

Test plan: compare the confrontational postponed-story message with a positive invitation to shared reading, using the same book, preview route and purchase terms. Record previews and paid orders, plus complaints, replies and refund requests where attributable. No campaign has been launched. The explicit instruction overrides the Hebrew skill's no-guilt clause for this scoped experiment; all other applicable copy constraints remain.

Proposed brand belief: a story can be a personal way to express care. The product must make that belief tangible through the child's illustrated likeness, name, a relevant story, and the experience of reading together. The existing brief describes photo and name personalisation and stories organised around occasions and childhood changes. ## 3. ICP: whom to prioritise

Recommended first ICP hypothesis: an adult choosing a personal gift for a child for an approaching birthday, who wants the gift to communicate that they know this particular child.

Qualification: a real occasion, interest in a personalised book, willingness to provide a suitable photo and name, and enough time to receive the chosen format. Verify actual production and delivery constraints before targeting urgent occasions.

Second ICP hypothesis: a parent looking for a story to read together around a new sibling, pacifier goodbye, potty training, or starting school. Treat emotional and behavioural benefits as hypotheses, not product guarantees.

Keep group buyers such as kindergartens in a separate sales route; the brief describes a different ordering process. [Product brief](/Users/yarinbruner/Claude/Projects/Lumebook/docs/lumebook-product-brief.md)

Do not equate ICP with “mothers aged 25 to 40.” Select by buying situation and fit; use demographics only where verified and useful.

## 4. Personas: the person inside that situation

These are research hypotheses, not fictional biographies presented as facts.

| Candidate buyer | Moment | Proposed inner sentence | Barrier to investigate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent buying a birthday gift | Birthday approaching | “I want this to feel like it was chosen just for them” | Will the likeness and finished book meet expectations? |
| Grandparent, aunt, or other close gift-giver | Birthday or family occasion | “I want my care to be felt when they open it” | Can I get the photo and complete the order easily? |
| Parent accompanying a change | New sibling or another relevant transition | “I want a way to talk about this together” | Will the actual story fit our situation? |
| Kindergarten teacher or group organiser | A group celebration or farewell, proposed occasions from Yarin | “I want each child to receive something personal from our time together” | Can I coordinate the photos, budget and delivery? |

For each validated persona, record buyer, recipient, occasion, trigger, desired progress, exact customer language, alternatives considered, objection, and purchase outcome. Add age or household details only if they change a marketing decision.

## 5. Job to be done and pain points

Gift job hypothesis: “When an important day is coming, help me give something personal that shows this child I know and love them.”

Transition job hypothesis: “When something is changing in our family, help me start a conversation through a story we can read together.”

| Layer | Gift route hypothesis | Transition route hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| Practical problem | Choosing a gift that fits this child | Finding a relevant story |
| Emotional tension | Wanting care to feel personal | Wanting to accompany the child well |
| Desired feeling | Recognition and closeness | Connection and a sense of possibility |
| Purchase friction | Likeness, print quality, total cost, arrival | Story fit, quality, total cost, exaggerated benefit claims |

Kindergarten job hypothesis: “Help me mark a shared occasion with a personal gift for each child, within the group's budget and deadline.” Investigate who chooses, pays and coordinates; do not assume all three are the teacher.

Parent job, made concrete: “When a new baby is coming, help me introduce what is changing through a story we can read together.” Proposed deeper buyer need: a sense of capability, “I have something concrete I can do with my child.” This is a motivation hypothesis, not a claim that the book changes behaviour.

Other parent jobs to investigate separately: an imaginative reading experience with no developmental problem, or a story around a specific transition such as giving up the pacifier. Do not assume every parent is looking for a tool.

Validate all four layers separately. Do not infer an emotional motive from buying a birthday title.

## 6. Positioning and promise

Proposed category: personalised children's storybooks.

Proposed umbrella positioning: **LumeBook creates personalised storybooks that put the child at the centre of an illustrated adventure or a story about a change in their life.** Product mechanisms and catalogue directions come from the [product brief](/Users/yarinbruner/Claude/Projects/Lumebook/docs/lumebook-product-brief.md); emotional meaning and buyer motivations are hypotheses.

Working product promise: the child appears as the hero through their name and illustrated likeness, as described in the [product brief](/Users/yarinbruner/Claude/Projects/Lumebook/docs/lumebook-product-brief.md). Proposed emotional expression: “Give them a story they can picture themselves in.” Avoid claiming the book will make a child brave, ready for a sibling, or successfully weaned.

Candidate alternatives to research: an ordinary book, a toy, another personalised gift, a story made at home, or doing nothing. These are a research list, not a verified competitive landscape. Establish what customers actually considered before claiming differentiation.

## 7. Proof and reasons to believe

Build a proof library with permission and provenance:

- Finished printed books, interiors, size and cover shown clearly.
- A real preview compared with the delivered book.
- The personalisation visible on actual pages.
- Customer words about why they bought, with context.
- Genuine child reactions where consent allows use; never stage a testimonial or infer a developmental result from a smile.
- Current product, total-price and delivery information.

Separate product proof from outcome proof. The existing brief's confidence and developmental language is brand positioning, not independently established efficacy. [Product brief](/Users/yarinbruner/Claude/Projects/Lumebook/docs/lumebook-product-brief.md)

Do not promise confidence gains, successful weaning, or reduced fear without evidence for the specific claim.

## 9. Offer and conversion path

Proposed journey: relevant occasion content -> matching story/product page -> create and inspect preview -> understand final format, total price and delivery -> purchase -> receive -> optional review or referral.

For each step define what the buyer needs to believe and provide the corresponding proof. Treat price surprise, unclear quality, and timing uncertainty as possible objections to investigate, not established explanations for abandonment.

## 11. Research and learning loop

Recommended first research round: five recent gift buyers, five buyers of transition stories, and five people who made a preview without purchasing. This is a proposed qualitative sample, not a statistically representative study. No outreach has been sent.

Ask about the actual event: why then, who the gift was for, what else they considered, what convinced them, what almost stopped them, and what happened after receipt. For non-buyers, ask what they did instead. Capture consented, anonymised language and distinguish observation from interpretation.

First gift message test: compare personal recognition with hero delight/possibility within the same birthday offer. Keep product, audience route and call to action comparable. A separate parent test can compare relevance of the story with the proposed sense of capability, within one transition such as a new sibling.

First practical scorecard: campaign identifier, buyer route, occasion, message hypothesis, call to action, unique link, previews started, paid orders, total acquisition spend if any, and customer words explaining interest or objection. Where unavailable, record unknown. For kindergarten enquiries, also record qualified enquiry, requested quantity, quote and paid group order. No measurement integration has been implemented by this strategy draft.

Measure the full path: qualified response, tracked visit where reliable, preview started, payment reached, paid order, and contribution after fulfilment and acquisition costs. Treat engagement as diagnostic. Mark unknown attribution as unknown. Current margin inputs remain an open question in the [sales notes](/Users/yarinbruner/Claude/Projects/Lumebook/docs/lumebook-sales-data-decisions-2026-10-12.md).

Choose the minimum practical improvement and test window before running a comparison. Low-volume results are directional; do not declare a winner from a few orders. Record evidence, decision and next test in this document.
